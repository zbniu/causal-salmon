# Effect of arrangement Q on recipients' change

The supplied study and data support an **average increase in change `z` for the units that received arrangement Q**. My adopted estimate of the average treatment effect on those 600 recipients is **1.21 points**, with an **approximate 95% bootstrap interval of 0.78 to 1.63 points**. This is the numerical result that answers the research question. It estimates how much larger their mean change was than it would have been if these same recipients had not received Q. It does not show that every recipient benefited, and it does not determine their exact individual counterfactual changes.

Only the supplied study description and the complete data.csv were used. The dataset contains 2,000 units: 600 recipients and 1,400 nonrecipients. All rows were retained, with no missing values or duplicate rows. Recipients' mean baseline was 66.84, compared with 57.23 for nonrecipients. Their observed mean changes were 12.83 and 9.38 points, respectively. The unadjusted difference of 3.45 points is a descriptive comparison, not the adopted causal estimate.

Baseline adjustment is necessary because the selection value increased with baseline, and the 600 available slots went to candidates with the highest baselines. This was not a simple randomized comparison between the two observed groups. However, the supplied description states that the random part of selection was unrelated to unit characteristics and that no characteristic other than baseline entered selection. Thus the described design supports comparing potential changes conditional on baseline. The missing selection values and candidate list do not, by themselves, introduce an additional unit characteristic requiring adjustment. The fixed numbers of candidates and slots do create dependence among assignments, and the supplied information is insufficient to reconstruct an exact randomization distribution. I rely on the stated absence of interference and switching when interpreting the outcome contrast causally.

I estimated the untreated mean-change function using all 1,400 nonrecipients, with a cubic B-spline regression on baseline. It had seven basis functions, boundaries at 45 and 75, and interior knots at 52.5, 60 and 67.5. I predicted each recipient's change without Q at that recipient's baseline and averaged observed minus predicted changes. This standardization targets the recipients rather than all 2,000 units, and does not require a constant treatment effect. The recipients' observed mean change of 12.83, minus their estimated mean change without Q of 11.61, gives the adopted 1.21-point estimate.

There is good observed baseline overlap for this target. Recipients' baselines range from 57.36 to 74.98, while nonrecipients' range from 45.01 to 75.00. No recipient lies outside the nonrecipients' baseline range. The median distance to the nearest nonrecipient baseline is 0.010 points and the largest is 0.116 points. Consequently, the estimate does not require predicting outside the observed untreated baseline range. This overlap supports estimation for recipients; it is not a claim of comparable treatment opportunities at every baseline in the whole study.

Checks using linear, quadratic and cubic regressions and splines with five and nine basis functions gave adjusted estimates of 1.17–1.21 points. Local linear comparisons using 5, 10, 20 or 40 nearest nonrecipient baselines gave 1.04–1.19 points. Comparisons within baseline strata of widths 0.5, 1 or 2 points, weighted to the recipients' distribution, gave 1.07–1.18 points. These are sensitivity checks, not additional adopted answers. They support the positive direction and a magnitude around one point. Ten-fold control-outcome prediction checks gave very similar errors across the regression specifications, approximately 3.58 points.

The reported interval comes from 2,000 bootstrap resamples, resampling recipients and nonrecipients separately and refitting the untreated spline each time (seed 872091). An approximate sandwich calculation gave a similar interval, 0.80 to 1.63 points. These intervals use conventional independent-unit sampling/model approximations; they are not exact design-based intervals for the fixed-slot allocation. Estimation also requires that the untreated mean-change relationship can be adequately approximated across nearby baselines. Agreement across flexible regression, local comparisons and stratification supports, but cannot prove, that modeling assumption. Subject to these stated limits, the evidence supports Q increasing recipients' average change by about 1.21 points. The supplied material cannot establish an exact finite-sample causal effect or effects for units that did not receive Q.
