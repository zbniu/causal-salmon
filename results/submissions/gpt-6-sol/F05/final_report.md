# Final report

**Answer.** The randomized study provides strong evidence that arrangement Q increased the *average* change `z` among the 600 units that received it. My adopted estimate for those units is an increase of **1.01 points** on the measurement scale (baseline-adjusted 95% confidence interval **0.65 to 1.38 points**). This is an estimate of their average causal effect, not an observed effect for each individual unit.

All 2,000 records were analyzed (600 received Q; 1,400 did not). Mean observed change was 10.759 points in the Q group and 9.911 points in the control group. The direct randomized difference was **0.848 points** (95% Welch interval 0.428 to 1.268; two-sided p = 0.000081). Baseline means were 59.158 and 59.910, respectively. Because baseline `y` preceded assignment and predicts change, I also fitted a linear regression of `z` on assignment, centered baseline, and their interaction. Averaging its Q-minus-control predictions over the treated units' baselines gives the adopted 1.014-point estimate; its HC3 robust standard error is 0.186. A 20,000-draw complete-randomization relabeling test for the sharp no-effect null found no differences as extreme as the observed unadjusted difference (Monte Carlo two-sided p < 0.00005).

Random assignment, receipt as assigned, complete records, common measurement procedures, blinded follow-up recording, and no effects across units make a causal interpretation credible for the study units. The exact counterfactual change of the treated units cannot be observed, so their exact average effect and individual effects are not known. The interval reflects sampling/randomization uncertainty in the estimate; the adjusted estimate additionally uses a linear baseline relationship, while the unadjusted comparison does not need that model.
